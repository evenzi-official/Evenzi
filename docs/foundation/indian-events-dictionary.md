## 1. Introduction

This document is a living reference guide to the sub-events, rituals, and cultural terms that make up Indian weddings and celebrations. It is written for anyone who may be unfamiliar with Indian traditions — whether you are a new team member at Evenzi, an international partner, an investor trying to understand our market, or a vendor who works with diverse clients.

**Who this is for:**
- Evenzi team members (especially those from non-Indian backgrounds)
- Admin, Operations, and Customer Success staff handling event queries
- Marketing and Content teams writing about Indian weddings
- International investors and partners learning about our market
- Eventually: guests viewing an event website who want to understand what they've been invited to

**How to use this guide:**
Read the overview first to understand the scale and structure of Indian celebrations. Then navigate by wedding type to find the specific sub-events relevant to your context. The glossary in Section 4 covers terms that appear across multiple traditions.

**An important note on diversity:**
India is home to dozens of religions, hundreds of regional cultures, and thousands of years of evolving tradition. There is no single "Indian wedding" — a Punjabi Hindu wedding looks very different from a Tamil Brahmin ceremony, which in turn differs from a Mumbai Muslim Nikah or a Goa Christian wedding. This guide covers the most commonly encountered traditions. Actual events on Evenzi are always customised per family — what is listed here is a starting point, not a rulebook.

## 2. Indian Wedding Overview

### Why Indian Weddings Are Unique

Indian weddings are not a single event — they are a series of ceremonies, rituals, and celebrations spread across multiple days, involving multiple families, and touching multiple generations. A wedding in India is as much a community event as it is a personal one. It is common for an entire extended family — grandparents, cousins, aunts, uncles, family friends — to be active participants, not just guests.

This multi-event structure is deeply intentional. Each ceremony serves a distinct purpose: some are religious and solemn, some are joyful and festive, and some are deeply emotional. Together they mark the transition of two individuals and two families into a new bond.

### Typical Scale

| Dimension | Range |
|-----------|-------|
| Total guests | 200 to 2,000 (large ceremonies can exceed this) |
| Duration | 2 to 7 days of events |
| Number of sub-events | 4 to 15+ depending on tradition |
| Venues | Often 2 to 5 different venues across the event period |
| Families coordinating | Minimum 2 (bride's and groom's); often extended joint families |

### The Planning Complexity

Coordinating an Indian wedding means managing multiple overlapping logistical threads simultaneously:

- Different sub-events may have different guest lists (some events are intimate family-only; others are open to 1,000+ guests)
- Different dates, times, and venues for each sub-event
- Catering, decor, photography, and entertainment vendors for each event
- Travel and accommodation coordination for outstation guests
- Ritual timing governed by astrological calendars (the auspicious time, called *Muhurat* or *Lagan*, is chosen by a priest and is non-negotiable)
- Dress codes may vary by event (Indian traditional for some events, Indo-Western for others)

This is precisely why Evenzi exists. The complexity of managing all of this in spreadsheets, WhatsApp groups, and phone calls is immense — and it is the everyday reality for millions of Indian families planning events.

## 3. Sub-Events by Wedding Type

---

### 3.1 Hindu Weddings — North India

North Indian Hindu weddings (covering states like Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, Delhi, and parts of Madhya Pradesh) are among the most elaborate and widely recognised Indian wedding formats globally. They typically span 3 to 5 days and involve a rich mix of pre-wedding, wedding day, and post-wedding events.

---

#### Roka / Sagai (Engagement)
**Pronunciation:** ROH-kah / SUH-gaee
**English equivalent:** Engagement ceremony

**What it is:** Roka is the formal moment when both families "stop" (the word *roka* literally means "to stop") the bride and groom from looking for other matches. It is the families' mutual declaration: *we have found the right match.* Gifts, sweets, and blessings are exchanged, and the couple is publicly acknowledged as engaged. In some families, this is a simple, intimate affair; in others, it is a large gathering with full catering and a DJ.

**Who attends:** Immediate family and close relatives of both sides. Typically 30–150 people.

**When:** Weeks to months before the wedding.

**Duration:** 2–4 hours.

**Venue type:** Family home, banquet hall, or a hotel.

---

#### Tilak / Sagan
**Pronunciation:** TI-luck / SAA-gun

**What it is:** The Tilak is a ceremony hosted by the bride's family at which the groom is formally welcomed and blessed. A red tilak (mark) is applied to the groom's forehead, gifts are presented to him by the bride's family, and sweets are distributed. It is a moment of respect and affection — the groom is being formally welcomed into a new family. In some traditions this is combined with the Roka; in others it is a separate event.

**Who attends:** Primarily the groom's family visiting the bride's family's home. 30–100 people.

**When:** A few days to weeks before the wedding.

**Duration:** 2–3 hours.

**Venue type:** Bride's family home or a hall.

---

#### Mehendi / Mehndi
**Pronunciation:** MEH-hen-dee

**What it is:** The Mehendi ceremony is a celebration of artistry, femininity, and joy. Henna — a natural dye made from the dried leaves of the henna plant — is applied to the bride's hands and feet in intricate patterns by a professional mehndi artist. The deeper and longer-lasting the mehndi colour, the saying goes, the more the groom loves the bride. It is an evening of music, laughter, and dance, with female relatives and friends of both families gathering around the bride. Many families now do a full "mehendi night" with a live band, food, and dancing. Commonly, the groom's name is hidden within the bride's mehendi design — a fun tradition where the groom must find it on their wedding night.

**Who attends:** Primarily women — female relatives and friends from both families. The groom sometimes makes a brief appearance. 50–300 guests.

**When:** 2–3 days before the wedding.

**Duration:** 4–8 hours (it is an evening event).

**Venue type:** Family home, outdoor lawn, or a banquet hall.

---

#### Haldi
**Pronunciation:** HAL-dee

**What it is:** Haldi is one of the most intimate and emotionally resonant pre-wedding ceremonies. A paste made of turmeric (haldi), sandalwood, and rose water is applied to the bride's (and sometimes the groom's, separately) skin by family members. Turmeric is known for its anti-inflammatory properties and its ability to give skin a warm, golden glow — but its significance goes far beyond skincare. It is a purification ritual, a blessing, and a moment when the family quite literally touches and tends to the bride before she leaves them. The mood is a mix of laughter (turmeric stains everything yellow) and tenderness. It is usually not a "dressed-up" event — family members wear old clothes they don't mind ruining.

**Who attends:** Immediate family and very close relatives. An intimate gathering of 15–60 people.

**When:** Morning of the wedding day, or the day before.

**Duration:** 1–3 hours.

**Venue type:** Family home.

---

#### Sangeet
**Pronunciation:** SUN-geet (the "g" is hard, as in "get")

**What it is:** The Sangeet is, for many families, the most anticipated and festive event of the entire wedding week. *Sangeet* means "music sung together," and that is exactly what it is — an evening of song, dance, and performances by both families. Family members (and sometimes friends) prepare choreographed dance performances to popular Bollywood songs in the weeks leading up to the wedding. There are friendly competitions between the bride's and groom's sides. Professional singers, DJs, or live bands often perform. The couple is celebrated, teased affectionately, and showered with love. If the Pheras (wedding ceremony) is the most sacred event, the Sangeet is the most joyful.

**Who attends:** Extended family and friends from both sides. 100–600 guests.

**When:** Evening, 1–2 days before the wedding.

**Duration:** 4–6 hours (evening event).

**Venue type:** Banquet hall, hotel ballroom, or outdoor venue with a stage.

---

#### Baraat
**Pronunciation:** Buh-RAAT

**What it is:** The Baraat is the groom's procession to the wedding venue — and it is one of the most visually spectacular events in an Indian wedding. The groom traditionally arrives on a decorated white horse (or sometimes an elephant, or in modern times a luxury car or even a helicopter). His family and friends dance around him in the street to dhol (drum) beats and brass band music, celebratory fireworks go off, and the whole procession moves towards the wedding venue in a slow, jubilant parade. The Baraat is a statement: the groom arrives as a king. The mood is euphoric.

**Who attends:** Groom's family and friends — the entire groom's guest contingent. 100–600 people.

**When:** Evening of the wedding day, before the ceremony begins.

**Duration:** 1–3 hours (including travel).

**Venue type:** Starts near the groom's home or hotel; ends at the wedding venue.

---

#### Milni
**Pronunciation:** MIL-nee

**What it is:** As the Baraat arrives at the wedding venue, the bride's family formally receives them in a ceremony called the Milni. The word means "meeting" — and it is a formal, choreographed meeting of equivalent family members from both sides. The bride's father meets the groom's father, the brothers meet each other, the maternal uncles meet each other, and so on. They exchange garlands and embrace. It is warm, sometimes humorous (as sisters-in-law traditionally tease and play pranks on the groom), and deeply symbolic of two families becoming one.

**Who attends:** Both families together at the wedding venue entrance. All wedding guests witness it.

**When:** Immediately when the Baraat arrives at the wedding venue.

**Duration:** 30–60 minutes.

**Venue type:** At the entrance of the wedding venue.

---

#### Wedding Ceremony / Pheras
**Pronunciation:** FAY-ruz

**What it is:** The Pheras are the heart and soul of a Hindu wedding — the moment when two people become husband and wife under sacred Vedic rites. A Pandit (Hindu priest) conducts the ceremony under a decorated canopy called the Mandap, around a sacred fire (Agni), which serves as the divine witness. The couple takes seven circles around the fire (the Saptapadi), each circle representing a vow — for nourishment, strength, prosperity, happiness, progeny, lifelong togetherness, and friendship. The groom ties the Mangalsutra (sacred necklace) around the bride's neck, and applies sindoor (vermillion powder) to the parting of her hair. These two acts — the Mangalsutra and the sindoor — are the definitive markers of a married Hindu woman. The ceremony is conducted in Sanskrit, and a good Pandit will translate and explain each ritual as it happens.

**Who attends:** All wedding guests. 200–2,000 people.

**When:** At the auspicious time (Muhurat) determined by the priest — often very early morning (4–8 AM) or evening.

**Duration:** 1.5–3 hours.

**Venue type:** Wedding lawn, hotel ballroom, or a temple hall, with a decorated Mandap at the centre.

---

#### Vidai
**Pronunciation:** Vi-DAA-ee

**What it is:** The Vidai is the moment the bride formally departs from her family home to begin her life in her husband's home. It is perhaps the most emotionally charged moment of the entire wedding. The bride throws handfuls of rice or flower petals over her shoulder as she leaves — a symbol of prosperity she leaves behind for her family. She weeps; her parents weep; everyone weeps. It is a moment of profound beauty and grief — the family celebrates her new life while simultaneously mourning the absence of their daughter. Even guests who have been celebrating all week are often moved to tears during the Vidai. It is customary for the bride not to look back.

**Who attends:** Family and close friends. The intimate core of both families.

**When:** After the wedding ceremony concludes, as the couple departs.

**Duration:** 30–60 minutes.

**Venue type:** The wedding venue or the bride's family home.

---

#### Reception
**Pronunciation:** (English — no translation needed)

**What it is:** The Reception is the post-wedding celebration, often hosted the next day or even a week later by the groom's family. It is typically the largest event in terms of guest count — the broader social network of both families is invited, including colleagues, business associates, and distant relatives who were not at the core ceremonies. The format is more relaxed and modern: dinner is served, there is a DJ or live music, and the newly married couple is formally introduced to the groom's extended network. Guests bring gifts and congratulate the couple.

**Who attends:** The broadest guest list of any single event. 300–2,000 guests.

**When:** Evening, 1–7 days after the wedding ceremony.

**Duration:** 4–6 hours.

**Venue type:** Large banquet hall or hotel.

---

### 3.2 Hindu Weddings — South India

South Indian Hindu weddings (covering Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, and Telangana) have their own distinct set of rituals, often conducted in Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, or Malayalam. While many themes are shared with North Indian weddings (sacred fire, seven steps, auspicious timing), the ceremonies have a different aesthetic — they tend to be morning events, and the Mandap (here called *Mantapam*) is set up differently. South Indian weddings are often more classically structured, with the priest playing a more central role throughout.

---

#### Muhurtham (Auspicious Time)
**Pronunciation:** Moo-HOOR-tum

**What it is:** Muhurtham is not an event in itself — it is the specific auspicious moment in time, determined by a Vedic astrologer or priest, during which the wedding ceremony must take place. Every element of the wedding — from the tying of the Thali to the first step around the sacred fire — must be completed within this window. Missing the Muhurtham is considered highly inauspicious. This is why South Indian weddings often begin very early in the morning (sometimes 6 AM or earlier) and proceed with careful timing. The Muhurtham governs the entire ceremony structure.

**Note for event planning:** When creating a South Indian wedding in Evenzi, the Muhurtham time is the non-negotiable anchor around which all other timings are built.

---

#### Nischayathartham (Engagement)
**Pronunciation:** Ni-SHCHA-ya-TAR-tum

**What it is:** The South Indian equivalent of the North Indian Roka, the Nischayathartham is the formal engagement ceremony. Both families gather, the Pandit/Purohit conducts a short ritual, and the couple formally accepts the match in the presence of family and God. Gifts, sweets, and new clothes are exchanged. It is a relatively simple ceremony compared to the wedding itself, but it is the official beginning of the wedding journey.

**Who attends:** Both families and close relatives. 50–200 people.

**When:** Weeks to months before the wedding.

**Duration:** 2–4 hours.

**Venue type:** Family home, community hall, or hotel.

---

#### Sumangali Pooja
**Pronunciation:** Soo-MUN-guh-lee POO-juh

**What it is:** Sumangali Pooja is a ritual performed to honour married women (*sumangalis*) who have passed away — essentially, the married female ancestors of the family. It is a deeply reverent ceremony that recognises the continuity of family life across generations. The living sumangalis of the family — grandmothers, aunts, mothers — are also honoured and blessed. It is a solemn, spiritually significant event that grounds the wedding in the context of the family's lineage and the divine blessings of those who came before.

**Who attends:** Immediate family, especially married women of the family.

**When:** Day before or morning of the wedding.

**Duration:** 1–2 hours.

**Venue type:** Family home or marriage hall.

---

#### Naandi
**Pronunciation:** NAAN-dee

**What it is:** Naandi is a ritual performed at the beginning of all major ceremonies in South Indian Hindu tradition. It is an invocation of the ancestors — a request for their blessings and permission before a significant life event begins. The priest conducts this ritual, and offerings are made to the family's forebears. Without Naandi, no major ceremony formally begins. Think of it as the opening prayer that connects the present moment to generations past, asking for their blessings to flow forward.

**Who attends:** Immediate family.

**When:** Morning before each major ceremony.

**Duration:** 30–60 minutes.

**Venue type:** Family home or marriage hall.

---

#### Kashi Yatra
**Pronunciation:** KAA-shee YAA-tra

**What it is:** Kashi Yatra is one of the most charming and humorous rituals in a South Indian wedding, and it offers a moment of genuine lightness within an otherwise serious ceremony. The groom, dressed in simple clothes and carrying a walking stick, umbrella, and a bundle of belongings, announces that he is renouncing worldly life and heading to Kashi (Varanasi) to become a monk. Just as he is about to "leave," the bride's father intercepts him, tells him that the path of a householder (grihastha) is equally noble, and convinces him to return by offering him his daughter's hand in marriage. It is theatrical, sometimes hilarious (especially when the family plays it up), and symbolically profound — the groom acknowledges that he is choosing family life willingly, understanding its duties and joys.

**Who attends:** All wedding guests.

**When:** During the wedding ceremony, before the main rituals.

**Duration:** 15–30 minutes.

**Venue type:** At the wedding venue.

---

#### South Indian Wedding Ceremony
**What it is:** The South Indian wedding ceremony shares the sacred core of the North Indian ceremony — the sacred fire, the seven steps (*Saptapadi*) — but has its own distinct rituals. Notable elements include the exchange of garlands (*Varmala*), the tying of the *Thali* (the sacred thread/necklace, equivalent to the North Indian Mangalsutra) around the bride's neck at the precise Muhurtham moment, and three circles around the sacred fire (not seven, in some traditions). The ceremony is conducted in Sanskrit or in the regional classical language. A distinctive feature of many South Indian ceremonies is the central role of the maternal uncle (*Mama*), who is traditionally responsible for giving the bride away and has specific duties in multiple rituals.

**Who attends:** All wedding guests. 200–1,500 people.

**When:** At the Muhurtham time — often 6–10 AM.

**Duration:** 2–4 hours.

**Venue type:** Marriage hall (*Kalyana Mandapam*) or hotel.

---

#### Gruhapravesh (Bride's Welcome)
**Pronunciation:** GROO-huh-PRA-vesh

**What it is:** Gruhapravesh literally means "entering the home," and it is the ceremony marking the bride's first entry into her new home. She steps over a threshold with her right foot, knocking over a vessel of rice that spills into the house — a symbol that she brings abundance with her. She is welcomed with arti (a ritual of light), blessings from the elders, and sometimes a formal puja. It is a tender moment that marks the beginning of her life in a new household.

**Who attends:** The groom's immediate family.

**When:** When the couple arrives at the groom's home after the wedding.

**Duration:** 30–60 minutes.

**Venue type:** The groom's family home.

---

### 3.3 Muslim Weddings

Muslim weddings in India blend Islamic religious requirements with regional cultural traditions. A Muslim wedding in Hyderabad looks different from one in Kashmir or Lucknow or Mumbai, but the core religious rituals are universal across the Islamic world. Indian Muslim weddings are often celebrated across 2–4 days, with a mix of solemn religious ceremony and vibrant cultural celebration.

---

#### Nikah
**Pronunciation:** NI-kuh

**What it is:** The Nikah is the Islamic marriage contract — the religious and legal union of the couple. It is conducted by a Qazi (Islamic officiant), in the presence of witnesses, and with the consent of both the bride and the groom. The groom proposes (*Ijab*), the bride accepts (*Qabool*), and this exchange of consent — verbal or written — is the binding act of marriage in Islam. The Mahr (also written Meher), a mandatory gift of money or property from the groom to the bride, is agreed upon and recorded in the Nikah contract. The Mahr belongs entirely to the bride and is her right. Many Nikahs are preceded by a reading from the Quran and a sermon (Khutba) by the Qazi.

**Who attends:** Immediate family and religious witnesses. Can be intimate (20 people) or a large gathering (200+), depending on family preference.

**When:** The central day of the wedding.

**Duration:** 1–2 hours for the religious ceremony.

**Venue type:** Mosque, family home, banquet hall, or hotel.

---

#### Mehndi (Muslim)
**Pronunciation:** MEH-hen-dee

**What it is:** Virtually identical to the Hindu Mehendi, the Muslim Mehndi is a pre-wedding celebration where henna is applied to the bride. The mood is festive and musical — Qawwali music or popular songs play, women dance, and food is shared. It is a beautiful cultural tradition rather than a religious one, and it crosses seamlessly across the Hindu-Muslim boundary.

**Who attends:** Female relatives and friends. 50–200 people.

**When:** 1–2 days before the Nikah.

**Duration:** 3–6 hours.

**Venue type:** Family home or banquet hall.

---

#### Barat / Baraat (Muslim)
**Pronunciation:** Buh-RAAT

**What it is:** The Muslim Baraat is functionally similar to the Hindu Baraat — the groom's procession to the wedding venue. The groom arrives with his family and friends in a celebratory procession. The level of musical accompaniment varies by family and region; some families prefer a more subdued entry in keeping with Islamic sensibility, while others have a full brass band and dancing.

**Who attends:** The groom's family and friends.

**When:** Before the Nikah ceremony.

**Duration:** 1–2 hours.

**Venue type:** Procession to the wedding venue.

---

#### Walima
**Pronunciation:** WAA-li-muh

**What it is:** The Walima is the wedding reception in Islamic tradition, hosted by the groom's family, and it is specifically sunnat (recommended practice by the Prophet Mohammed) to celebrate the marriage publicly. It is a feast of thanksgiving — a generous spread of food shared with family, friends, and often the wider community. The Walima is a joyful, social occasion: guests congratulate the couple, share meals, and celebrate. In many Indian Muslim families, the Walima has grown into a large, elaborate event rivalling the Nikah in scale.

**Who attends:** Extended family and friends, groom's network. 200–1,000 guests.

**When:** Day after the Nikah, or within 7 days.

**Duration:** 4–6 hours.

**Venue type:** Banquet hall or hotel.

---

### 3.4 Sikh Weddings

Sikh weddings (called *Anand Karaj*, meaning "blissful union") are celebrated with deep reverence for the Guru Granth Sahib (the Sikh holy scripture) and with exuberant Punjabi cultural energy. The ceremony takes place in a Gurdwara (Sikh house of worship), and the rituals are equal in their demands on both bride and groom — there is no "giving away" of the bride in Sikhism; she is an equal participant throughout.

---

#### Roka (Sikh)
**Pronunciation:** ROH-kah

**What it is:** The Sikh Roka is similar to the Hindu Roka — a formal meeting of the families to accept the match. Prayers are offered, sweets are distributed, and the families commit to the union. It is typically a warm, informal gathering at one family's home.

**Who attends:** Both families and close relatives.

**When:** Months before the wedding.

**Duration:** 2–3 hours.

**Venue type:** Family home or Gurdwara.

---

#### Chunni Ceremony
**Pronunciation:** CHUN-nee

**What it is:** The Chunni Ceremony is specific to Sikh and some Punjabi Hindu weddings, and it is a beautiful gesture of formal acceptance. The groom's mother (or a senior female relative) visits the bride's home and drapes a red dupatta (head covering/scarf) over the bride's head. This single act carries profound meaning: the groom's family is publicly declaring that they accept this woman as their daughter-in-law. The bride is welcomed into a new family with this simple but deeply symbolic gesture. Gifts, sweets, and blessings follow.

**Who attends:** The groom's family visiting the bride's family. 20–100 people.

**When:** Weeks to months before the wedding.

**Duration:** 2–3 hours.

**Venue type:** Bride's family home.

---

#### Mehendi (Sikh)
The Sikh Mehendi is essentially the same as the Hindu Mehendi ceremony — a festive, music-filled evening of henna application. Punjabi folk music (Gidda for women, Bhangra for mixed gatherings) adds to the celebration. *See Section 3.1 for full details.*

---

#### Sangeet (Sikh)
The Sikh Sangeet mirrors the Hindu Sangeet in structure — family dance performances, live music, and celebration. Bhangra and Gidda are the characteristic dance forms. *See Section 3.1 for full details.*

---

#### Anand Karaj (The Sikh Wedding Ceremony)
**Pronunciation:** UH-nund KAA-raaj

**What it is:** Anand Karaj is the Sikh wedding ceremony, conducted in the Gurdwara in the presence of the Guru Granth Sahib. The ceremony involves four *Laavan* — four rounds taken by the couple around the Guru Granth Sahib, walking clockwise, as the corresponding verse from the scripture is read aloud and sung by the Ragis (religious musicians). Each Laavan marks a stage in the couple's spiritual journey together. Before the Laavan, the Ardas (Sikh prayer) is offered, and the Lavan Anand Sahib (hymn of bliss) is recited. The atmosphere in the Gurdwara is one of serene joy — heads covered, seated on the ground as equals before the scripture, with the sound of Kirtan (devotional music) filling the hall.

**Who attends:** All guests. 100–800 people.

**When:** Morning (Gurdwaras are typically most active in the morning).

**Duration:** 1.5–2 hours for the ceremony.

**Venue type:** Gurdwara (Sikh place of worship).

---

#### Reception / Doli (Sikh)
**Pronunciation:** DOH-lee

**What it is:** The Doli marks the bride's departure from her family and arrival at her husband's home — an event filled with both joyful celebration and emotional farewell, much like the Hindu Vidai. Traditionally, the bride was carried in a *doli* (palanquin); today it is a procession by car. The Reception that follows is typically a large, lavish celebration with Punjabi food, Bhangra dancing, and a DJ. Sikh receptions are known for their energy and hospitality.

**Who attends:** Extended family and friends. 300–2,000 people.

**When:** Evening after the wedding ceremony.

**Duration:** 4–8 hours.

**Venue type:** Banquet hall or outdoor venue.

### 3.5 Christian Weddings — Indian

Indian Christian weddings blend the universal traditions of Christian marriage with Indian warmth, scale, and festivity. They are most common in Kerala (Syrian Christian, Catholic, and Protestant communities), Goa (Catholic), Tamil Nadu, and among Anglo-Indian communities. Indian Christian weddings tend to be highly organised, with a strong emphasis on family togetherness, and they often include Indian cultural elements alongside the church service.

---

#### Engagement
**What it is:** The engagement ceremony involves the formal exchange of rings between the couple in the presence of family, friends, and often a priest or pastor. In Kerala Christian tradition this can be quite an elaborate ceremony; in other communities it may be simpler. Prayers, blessings, and a celebratory meal follow. The engagement is the public declaration of the couple's intent to marry.

**Who attends:** Both families and close friends. 50–200 people.

**When:** Months before the wedding.

**Duration:** 2–4 hours.

**Venue type:** Church, family home, or banquet hall.

---

#### Rehearsal Dinner
**What it is:** The evening before the wedding, the wedding party (bridesmaids, groomsmen, close family) gathers for a rehearsal of the ceremony — walking through positions, cues, and order of events. This is followed by an intimate dinner, usually hosted by the groom's family, as a gesture of thanks to everyone who has travelled and participated. It is a relaxed, warm occasion that brings the core of both families together before the big day.

**Who attends:** The wedding party and immediate family. 20–60 people.

**When:** Evening before the wedding.

**Duration:** 3–5 hours.

**Venue type:** Restaurant, family home, or hotel.

---

#### Bridal Shower
**What it is:** The Bridal Shower is a celebration of the bride, hosted by her bridesmaids, close friends, or female relatives. Gifts (particularly items for the new home) are given to the bride, and the gathering involves games, food, and a lot of joyful affection for the bride-to-be. It is an intimate event that celebrates the bride's transition from her single life to marriage.

**Who attends:** Female friends and relatives of the bride. 15–50 people.

**When:** Days to weeks before the wedding.

**Duration:** 3–5 hours.

**Venue type:** Restaurant, someone's home, or a private venue.

---

#### Bachelor / Bachelorette Party
**What it is:** A celebration for the groom (bachelor party) or bride (bachelorette party) with their respective group of friends — traditionally the "last night of freedom," though in practice it is simply a fun gathering. In Indian contexts these are increasingly popular, especially in urban settings. They range from a dinner out to a weekend trip to Goa.

**Who attends:** The groom/bride's close friends. 10–30 people.

**When:** Night before or a few days before the wedding.

**Duration:** An evening or a weekend.

**Venue type:** Varies.

---

#### Wedding Ceremony (Christian)
**What it is:** The Christian wedding ceremony takes place in a church, presided over by a priest or pastor. The couple exchanges vows ("to have and to hold, from this day forward..."), rings are exchanged as a symbol of unending love, and the marriage is blessed and solemnised by the officiant. For Catholic weddings, the ceremony may be part of a full Mass; for Protestant weddings, it is typically a service with hymns, scripture readings, and a sermon. Indian Christian weddings often feel very communal — extended families fill the church, and there is a warmth and expressiveness that blends Indian family culture with Christian faith.

**Who attends:** All wedding guests. 100–500 people.

**When:** Mid-morning to noon (traditionally).

**Duration:** 1–2 hours.

**Venue type:** Church.

---

#### Reception (Christian)
**What it is:** The wedding reception follows the church ceremony, typically at a separate venue. It is a dinner-and-dancing celebration with toasts from the best man and maid of honour, the first dance, cake cutting, and a sit-down meal. Indian Christian receptions are known for their exceptional food — particularly Kerala-style receptions, where the spread of dishes is legendary. Guests congratulate the couple, and the evening is a joyful blend of Western reception format with Indian hospitality.

**Who attends:** All wedding guests, plus broader family and friend network. 150–800 people.

**When:** Afternoon/evening of the wedding day.

**Duration:** 4–6 hours.

**Venue type:** Hotel, banquet hall, or outdoor garden.

## 5. Event Types on Evenzi — Beyond Weddings

Evenzi is designed for all Indian celebrations, not just weddings. Here are the other major event types the platform supports, along with their sub-events.

---

### Milestone Birthdays

In Indian families, certain birthdays are not private affairs — they are community celebrations. The 1st birthday (the first year of life survived, once a milestone in itself), 18th, 21st, 50th, 60th (Shashtiabdhapoorthi in South India), and 75th (Sadabhishekam) birthdays are celebrated with the same scale and seriousness as weddings in many families.

**Typical sub-events:**
- **Puja / Prayer Ceremony** — A religious ritual at home or a temple, seeking blessings for the celebrant.
- **Cake Cutting Ceremony** — Western tradition now deeply embedded in Indian celebrations; often the centrepiece of the celebration with family gathered around.
- **Lunch or Dinner** — A catered meal for all guests; in South India, traditional banana-leaf meals are common for milestone birthdays.

**Typical scale:** 50–500 guests.

---

### Corporate Events

Evenzi serves corporate clients managing internal and client-facing events. Indian corporate culture has its own event vocabulary.

**Typical sub-events:**
- **Registration / Check-In** — Guest arrival, badge collection, networking.
- **Keynote / Presentations** — Main stage sessions, speaker slots.
- **Networking Sessions** — Structured or unstructured time for attendees to connect.
- **Award Ceremony** — Recognition of employees or partners; often the emotional peak of an annual party.
- **Gala Dinner** — Formal sit-down dinner, often with entertainment.

**Typical scale:** 50–1,000+ attendees.

---

### Other Celebrations

**Baby Shower / Godh Bharai**
*Pronunciation: GOHD Buh-RAA-ee* — Literally "filling the lap." A ceremony in which female family members place fruits, flowers, and sweets in the expecting mother's lap, symbolising abundance and blessings for the unborn child. It is a warm, intimate, female-centric celebration. Scale: 30–100 guests.

**Naming Ceremony / Namkaran**
*Pronunciation: NUM-kuh-run* — The ceremony in which a newborn baby receives their name. Conducted with a priest, family, and the naming is done in the presence of the sacred fire. Often the first major ceremony in a child's life. Scale: 30–150 guests.

**Thread Ceremony / Janeu / Upanayana**
*Pronunciation: JA-new / Oo-puh-NAA-yuh-nuh* — A rite of passage for young boys in Hindu families, typically between ages 7–11, marking their formal entry into the student phase of life. A sacred thread (janeu) is tied around the boy's body. It is the Hindu equivalent of a bar mitzvah in terms of social and religious significance.

**Housewarming / Griha Pravesh**
*Pronunciation: GREE-huh PRA-vesh* — The ceremony marking the first entry into a new home. A puja is performed, the home is blessed, and family and friends are invited to celebrate. In many Indian families, entering a new home without a proper Griha Pravesh is considered inauspicious. Scale: 50–200 guests.

**Retirement Parties**
Less formal than the above, but growing in scale in Indian professional culture. Often combined with a felicitation ceremony (honouring the retiree with a shawl, a momento, and speeches). Scale: 50–300 people.

## 6. How Evenzi Uses This

This dictionary is not just a reference guide — it is the foundation for how Evenzi thinks about and structures events on the platform.

### Sub-Events as First-Class Citizens

When a host creates a Wedding event on Evenzi, they are not just creating a single event. The platform guides them through creating a set of sub-events tailored to their wedding type. A Hindu wedding host from North India will see a suggested set of sub-events that includes Mehendi, Haldi, Sangeet, Baraat, Pheras, and Reception. A South Indian host will see a different default set. Each suggestion is a starting point — families can add, remove, rename, or reorder based on their own customs.

### Each Sub-Event Is Independent

Every sub-event on Evenzi has its own:
- Date and time
- Venue
- Invited guest list (a curated subset of the master guest list)
- RSVP tracking
- Event page with description, dress code, and schedule

This means guests receive targeted invitations — a work colleague of the groom might be invited only to the Reception, while close family is invited to all events. The system handles this automatically once the host assigns guests to sub-events.

### The Event Website as Cultural Bridge

Every Evenzi wedding generates a public event website with a sub-event timeline. International guests, colleagues who may be unfamiliar with Indian traditions, or anyone who receives an invitation can visit the event website and see, in plain language, what each sub-event is, what to expect, what to wear, and when to arrive.

**Planned for Phase 2:** Non-Indian guests viewing the event website will be able to hover or click on any sub-event name — "Sangeet," "Haldi," "Anand Karaj" — and see a brief, friendly explanation drawn from this very dictionary. The goal is to make every guest feel informed, welcomed, and excited — not confused.
